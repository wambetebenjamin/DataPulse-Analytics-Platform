We get called in to replace a lot of dashboards. Rarely because the underlying numbers were wrong — almost always because nobody was opening the thing. These are the recurring reasons.

## 1. Everything is on one screen

Thirty panels of equal visual weight means the reader must decide what matters, every single time they look. They will not. Pick the three to five numbers that drive decisions, make them large and put them at the top. Everything else goes below the fold or on a separate tab.

## 2. No comparison

"Revenue: KES 1.4M" is not information. Information is "KES 1.4M, up 12% on last month, against a target of 1.6M." Every headline figure needs a comparator — prior period, target, or both.

## 3. Pie charts with nine slices

The human eye cannot rank angles accurately past about five categories. Beyond that, use a horizontal bar chart sorted by value and the comparison becomes instant. Reserve donuts for two to five segments where the part-to-whole relationship is genuinely the point.

## 4. Colour used decoratively

If every chart is a different colour for variety, colour carries no meaning. Fix a semantic palette — one colour for revenue, one for cost, red reserved exclusively for problems — and keep it identical across every panel. A reader should be able to glance at a hue and know what family of thing they are looking at.

## 5. Truncated y-axes

Starting the axis at 80% of the minimum value makes a 2% change look like a crisis. It is the most common way dashboards accidentally lie. Start at zero unless you have an explicit, stated reason not to.

## 6. Metrics with no definition

"Active customers" means four different things to four different people in the same company. Every calculated metric needs an expander explaining the formula and the window. We put a "How is this calculated?" control next to each derived figure, and it is one of the most-clicked elements we ship.

## 7. Forecasts drawn like facts

A projection rendered in the same solid line as actuals will be read as actuals. Dash the projected segment, shade the confidence band, and label it an estimate. This is not pedantry — it is the difference between a planning tool and a liability.

## 8. Mobile as an afterthought

In East Africa, most owners will open the dashboard on a phone. A table that needs pinch-zooming does not get read. Stack the KPI cards to a single column, let tables scroll horizontally with a visible affordance, and never hide critical information in a hover state that does not exist on touch.

## 9. No path from insight to action

The reader sees that stock of a top seller runs out in four days. Now what? If the answer involves opening three other systems, the insight evaporates. Put the action next to the number: a reorder link, a WhatsApp message to the supplier, an export for the meeting.

## The test

Give the dashboard to someone who did not build it, say nothing, and watch for sixty seconds. If they cannot tell you what the business should do differently this week, the dashboard has failed — regardless of how correct the figures are.
