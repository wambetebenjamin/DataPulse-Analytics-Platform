Most businesses we speak to assume the Data Protection Act 2019 is something for banks and telcos. It is not. If you keep a spreadsheet of customer phone numbers, you are a data controller and the Act applies to you. This is a practical orientation, not legal advice — for the latter, speak to a qualified advocate.

## The vocabulary that matters

**Personal data** is anything that identifies a living person: name, phone number, M-Pesa payer identifier, email, ID number, location, even a customer code if it can be linked back to a person.

**Data controller** is whoever decides why and how that data is processed. If you own the business, that is you.

**Data processor** is whoever processes it on your behalf. Your analytics platform, your email provider, your hosting company. You remain responsible for choosing processors who can meet the standard.

## You need a lawful basis

Processing must rest on one of the grounds in the Act. In practice, business dashboards usually rely on:

- **Performance of a contract** — you need the customer's phone number to deliver their order.
- **Legitimate interest** — analysing your own sales data to run the business, provided it does not override the customer's rights.
- **Consent** — required for marketing. It must be freely given, specific and withdrawable, which means a pre-ticked box is not consent.

Write down which basis applies to which processing activity. It takes an hour and it is the first thing anyone will ask for.

## Collect less

The Act requires data minimisation: collect only what you need for the stated purpose. The analytics implication is counter-intuitive but important — aggregate early. If your dashboard answers the question "how many customers bought twice this month", it does not need to hold the names. A hashed identifier is enough. Less stored personal data means less risk, less obligation and a simpler breach position.

## Set a retention period and honour it

"We keep everything forever" is not a retention policy. Decide, per category, how long you genuinely need the record — transaction records for the statutory accounting period, marketing contacts until consent is withdrawn, CVs for six months — and delete on schedule. Automate the deletion; manual purges never happen.

## Data subject rights you must be able to service

Within the statutory response window, a person can ask you to:

1. Confirm whether you hold their data and give them a copy.
2. Correct anything inaccurate.
3. Delete it, where there is no overriding basis to keep it.
4. Object to processing, including profiling.
5. Receive it in a portable, machine-readable format.

The operational question is simply: could you find every record relating to one phone number across every system you use, within a week? If the honest answer is no, that is the first thing to fix.

## Breach notification

A breach that poses a real risk to data subjects must be reported to the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, and the affected people must be told where the risk is high. Have the contact details and a one-page internal procedure written down *before* you need them.

## What to put in your privacy notice

Plain language, no legal thicket: what you collect, why, the lawful basis, who you share it with, how long you keep it, the rights listed above, and a named contact for data protection queries. If you process at scale or handle sensitive categories, you may need a registered Data Protection Officer — check your position against the registration thresholds.

## Choosing an analytics provider

Ask three questions. Where is the data stored and who can access it? Will you sign a processing agreement that limits use to providing the service? Can we export everything and have it deleted on exit? Any provider who hesitates on the third question is telling you something important.
